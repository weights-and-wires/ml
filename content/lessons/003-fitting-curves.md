# Fitting a curve: polynomials, least squares, and the degree dial

In the made-up market of the previous chapter, rent rises, falls, and rises again as size grows, so a straight line misses the bends. The memorising rule from the chapter "Honest scores: training error, test error, and holding data back" is the opposite problem. It follows every wobble in the rents, including the landlord's mood, and fails on flats it has not seen.

You want something in between: a curve that bends enough to follow the real pattern and not so much that it chases the wobble. Is there one dial that goes from stiff to wiggly? If there is, where on the dial is the best rule?

This chapter builds that dial from polynomials. You turn a curve into a short list of weights, fit the weights with least squares, and then turn the dial while you watch the training score and the held-out score.

## What you already know

You already use these ideas:

- Empirical risk is the average loss, and squared loss is the squared miss.
- A training score is too kind, so a rule is judged on flats it was not fit to.
- A power of a number multiplies it by itself: $2^3=2\times2\times2=8$, and any number to the power 0 is 1.
- A weighted sum such as `1*1 + 3*2 + 0.5*4` multiplies each value by a weight and adds the results.
- numpy writes a weighted sum of many rows as the matrix product `phi @ w`.

## One dial, many shapes

The data are the made-up market from the previous chapter. Size runs from 0 to 1, and rent is measured in hundreds of pounds above or below typical. This chapter fits 15 flats.

A *polynomial* of degree $p$ builds a guess from powers of the size $x$:

$$
\hat{y}=w_0+w_1x+w_2x^2+\dots+w_px^p
$$

Read it aloud: the guess is a constant $w_0$, plus $w_1$ times the size, plus $w_2$ times the size squared, and so on up to the $p$-th power. The numbers $w_0,\dots,w_p$ are the *weights*. They are the knobs. Degree 1 is a straight line, degree 2 allows one bend, and every extra degree allows more bends. Degree is the dial, and the weights are what you fit once the dial is set.

Take a tiny case. Let $p=2$ with weights 1, 3, and 0.5, and let the size be $x=2$. The powers are $2^0=1$, $2^1=2$, and $2^2=4$. The guess is $1\times1+3\times2+0.5\times4=1+6+2=9$.

## Turn a curve into a table

How do you find the weights? Look at the formula again. The guess is bent in $x$, but it is only a weighted sum of the numbers $1$, $x$, $x^2$, and so on. Each weight multiplies one of those numbers and nothing else, with no weight inside a power or a product with another weight. If you compute those numbers first and put them in columns, the curve problem turns into a plain weighted-sum problem: find one weight per column. The bend lives in the columns, and the weights stay simple.

Each flat becomes one row of the table. Column $j$ holds $x^j$. For sizes 2 and −1 at degree 2, row one is $1, 2, 4$. Row two is $(-1)^0, (-1)^1, (-1)^2$, which is $1, -1, 1$. This table is the *design matrix*, written $\phi$ (the Greek letter phi, said "fie"). Multiply it by the weight vector and you get one guess per row, as Figure 2 shows: 9 for the first flat and $1-3+0.5=-1.5$ for the second.

In numpy, one line builds every column:

```python
phi = x[:, None] ** np.arange(degree + 1)[None, :]   # shape (n, degree + 1), column j is x ** j
guesses = phi @ w                                    # shape (n,)
```

Two small traps live here. `np.arange(degree)` stops one power short and gives one column too few, so the shape is `(n, degree)`. And a size of 0 is safe: numpy computes `0.0 ** 0` as 1, so column 0 stays all ones, and a flat of size 0 still gets the constant $w_0$ as its guess.

> [!TIP]
> Print `phi.shape` right after you build it. It must read `(n, degree + 1)`.

## Choose the weights: least squares

Which weights? Use the squared-loss score you already know. In Figure 1 each red segment is the vertical miss between a flat and the curve, and the empirical risk with squared loss adds up their squares. *Least squares* picks the weights that make the sum of the squared vertical misses as small as possible. Dividing the sum by $n$ gives the empirical risk and does not move the minimum, so the two descriptions pick the same weights.

Why not scan, as in "Scoring a guess: loss and empirical risk"? There, one knob and nine candidate values meant nine scores. A degree-12 curve has 13 weights. Nine candidates for each would be $9^{13}$, about 2.5 trillion curves to score. You need a solver that goes straight to the best weights.

A derivation of the solution comes in a later chapter, "Fit a line by minimising squared misses". Until then, treat numpy's solver as a tool with one clear promise: it returns the weights with the smallest total squared miss. Check the promise on a case you can do by eye. The three points $(0,1)$, $(1,3)$, and $(2,5)$ lie exactly on the line $1+2x$, so the solver must return weights 1 and 2. It does. One more property of the answer, which the exercise also checks: at the least-squares weights, the misses are at right angles to every column of $\phi$, so `phi.T @ misses` is all zeros. That says no small nudge to the weights lowers the total miss.

```python
w = np.linalg.lstsq(phi, y, rcond=None)[0]   # lstsq returns a tuple; item 0 holds the weights
```

The usual mistake is to keep the whole tuple. The weights are the first item only.

To score a fitted polynomial, build the design matrix for the flats you want to guess and multiply by the weights. The starter provides `predict_polynomial` for that, so the code in the exercise stays short.

> [!KEY]
> A polynomial's degree is a dial from stiff to wiggly. The training risk falls as you turn it up, which hides the damage, and only a held-out score shows where extra wiggle stops helping and starts hurting.

> [!RECAP]
> - A polynomial of degree $p$ has $p+1$ weights. The design matrix has one column per power, shape `(n, p + 1)`.
> - The curve bends in $x$, but the guess is a weighted sum of columns, so a straight-line solver can fit it.
> - Least squares minimises the total squared vertical miss. `np.linalg.lstsq(...)[0]` holds the weights.
> - Training risk falls with degree. Held-out risk falls and then rises.
> - Choose the degree with the lowest held-out risk.

## Build it step by step

The starter file provides `squared_loss`, `empirical_risk`, and `train_test_split` from earlier chapters, and `predict_polynomial`, which depends on your `polynomial_features`. You write three functions.

1. **`polynomial_features(x, degree)`** takes `x` of shape `(n,)` and returns shape `(n, degree + 1)`, where column `j` is `x ** j`:

   ```python
   powers = np.arange(degree + 1)   # 0, 1, ..., degree
   # x[:, None] ** powers[None, :]
   ```

   `np.arange(degree)` leaves out the last power.

2. **`fit_polynomial(x, y, degree)`** returns the least-squares weights, shape `(degree + 1,)`:

   ```python
   phi = polynomial_features(x, degree)
   # np.linalg.lstsq(phi, y, rcond=None) returns a tuple. Keep item 0.
   ```

   The whole tuple is not the weight vector.

3. **`sweep_degrees(x_train, y_train, x_test, y_test, degrees)`** fits each degree on the training flats and returns two arrays, `train_risks` and `test_risks`, each of shape `(len(degrees),)`:

   ```python
   for degree in degrees:
       w = fit_polynomial(x_train, y_train, degree)
       # Score predict_polynomial(w, x_train) and predict_polynomial(w, x_test).
   ```

   Fit on the training flats only. Fitting on the test flats makes the held-out column meaningless.

## Where this breaks

**High powers are badly scaled.** In this market sizes lie between 0 and 1, so high powers shrink: $0.5^{12}$ is about 0.0002. The high-degree columns are nearly zero for most flats and nearly alike, so the solver needs enormous weights to make them matter, as the 100-million weight above shows. If size were in square metres instead, a 50 square metre flat would give $50^{12}$, about $2\times10^{20}$, in the same table as a column of ones. Either way the fit is fragile. Rescale the sizes before you use high powers.

**Too many weights for too few flats.** With 15 flats, a degree-14 polynomial has 15 weights and can pass through every flat exactly, like the memoriser. Training risk of 0 tells you nothing.

**Choosing the degree by training risk.** It always picks the largest degree. Choose by held-out risk, on a pile of flats that no fit has seen.

**One held-out score.** The held-out risk at degree 3 and degree 6 differ by 0.008, too little to call one better on a single sample of flats. Pick a simple degree near the bottom of the curve instead of the exact minimum.

## The exercise

You build `polynomial_features`, `fit_polynomial`, and `sweep_degrees`. The checks confirm that the design matrix has one column per power, including the column of ones at size 0, and that your weights recover an exact polynomial and reproduce the guesses 9 and −1.5 from Figure 2.

They also verify that no nearby weights give a lower total squared miss, so the fit really is the minimum. Finally, `sweep_degrees` must give training risks that never rise with degree, a held-out risk that falls and then rises, and a huge held-out risk with a tiny training risk when the degree nearly equals the number of training flats.
