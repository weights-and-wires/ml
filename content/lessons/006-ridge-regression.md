# Ridge regression: a fee on large weights, for every lambda

You have two measurements. At input 0 the label is 0. At input 0.1 the label is 1. You fit a line through the origin, so there is one weight to find: the slope. The line that hits both points exactly has slope 10, and it predicts 10 at input 1. That is ten times the largest label you have ever seen.

The input is only 0.1, so the only way to reach a label of 1 from there is a huge slope. A large weight means a small change in the input swings the prediction by a lot. Two points cannot tell you whether the label of 1 is a real effect or noise. If it is noise, the slope of 10 is a disaster at input 1, and a flatter line such as slope 5 gives up half of that one label to stay sensible everywhere else. When the data cannot tell you whether a big swing is real, you want the model to hold back.

The standard way to make a model hold back is to charge a fee for large weights. That raises a second question. How big should the fee be? The only way to know is to try many values, and with 1,000 features each try is a linear solve of about a billion multiply-adds, although only the fee changes between tries and the data does not. This chapter builds the fee, solves for the bottom of its score, and then shows how one decomposition of the data gives the answer for every fee at once, and tells you which parts of the weights the data can pin down.

## What you already know

- Half the sum of squared misses scores a fit, and the best least-squares weights solve $X^\top X\, w = X^\top y$.
- A rule that matches the noise in its training labels does worse on unseen data, as in [bias, variance, and noise](/lessons/002-curves-and-bias-variance), and a held-out score picks between settings, as in [honest scores](/lessons/001-loss-and-honest-scores).
- The gradient points uphill, and at the bottom of a bowl it is zero, as in [gradient descent](/lessons/005-gradient-descent-and-momentum).
- You solve a linear system with a solver rather than an inverse, because the solver is faster and loses fewer digits.
- The SVD writes $X = U\,\mathrm{diag}(s)\,V^\top$: rotate the weights with $V^\top$, stretch each direction by its singular value $s_i$, then rotate into prediction space with $U$. A small singular value marks a direction the data barely tells apart, as in [stable least squares](/lessons/004-stable-least-squares).

## Add the fee to the score

Why not cap the weights, allowing none above 3? A cap stops the slope of 10, but it treats a weight of 2.9 and a weight of 0.1 the same, picks 3 for no reason the data gave, and breaks the smooth bowl that downhill methods rely on. A fee is softer. Every bit of weight costs something, a little at first and more as the weight grows. The data pulls the weight up to fit, the fee pulls it down, and the answer is where the pulls balance.

Take the two-point example and charge $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda w^2$, where $\lambda$ (lambda) is the fee rate, which you choose. Try $\lambda = 0.01$. The score of a slope $w$ is the half-squared misses plus the fee.

| Slope $w$ | Half-squared misses | Fee | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.5 | 0 | 0.5 |
| 1 | 0.405 | 0.005 | 0.41 |
| 5 | 0.125 | 0.125 | 0.25 |
| 10 | 0 | 0.5 | 0.5 |

The perfect fit at slope 10 is no longer the best. Slope 5 scores 0.25, half as much, because the misses form a bowl with its bottom at 10, the fee forms a bowl with its bottom at 0, and the sum of two bowls is a bowl with its bottom in between.

You can find that bottom by hand. The slope of the score is $0.1(0.1w - 1) + \lambda w$. Set it to zero and collect the terms: $(0.01 + \lambda)\,w = 0.1$. So the best slope is $0.1 / (0.01 + \lambda)$, which is 10 at $\lambda=0$, 5 at $0.01$, 0.91 at $0.1$, and 0.099 at 1. Figure 1 draws these lines and the slope as the fee grows.

The fee is a dial: at 0 you get plain least squares, and as it grows the weight shrinks smoothly towards 0. No setting is correct in general. You choose it by holding data back and keeping the fee whose fit scores best on that data.

## Read the ridge score aloud

With many weights, the fee charges the squared length of the weight vector $w$. The whole score is

$$
L(w) = \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert Xw - y\rVert^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2
$$

Read it as: half the squared misses, plus half of lambda times the squared length of the weights. The first half is the least-squares score. Both halves carry a one half so that, when you take the slope, the 2 in each square cancels it. Charging the *square* of each weight keeps the score a smooth bowl.

Check it on the smallest two-weight case, where $X$ is the identity, so the predictions are the weights, and $y = (3, 1)$. Take $\lambda = 1$. The weights $(3, 1)$ miss by 0, and their fee is $\tfrac12(9 + 1) = 5$, so they score 5. The weights $(1.5, 0.5)$ miss by $-1.5$ and $-0.5$, which costs $\tfrac12(2.25 + 0.25) = 1.25$, and their fee is also $1.25$. They score 2.5, half as much.

```python
misses = X @ w - y                       # shape (n,)
score = 0.5 * np.sum(misses ** 2) + 0.5 * lam * np.dot(w, w)
```

## Find the bottom with one linear solve

The gradient of the first half is $X^\top(Xw - y)$. The gradient of the fee is $\lambda w$, one pull per weight, proportional to the weight itself. So

$$
\nabla L(w) = X^\top(Xw - y) + \lambda w
$$

This is the data's pull plus the fee's pull. At the bottom of a bowl the gradient is zero, and because the score is a bowl there is exactly one such point. Set it to zero, $X^\top X w - X^\top y + \lambda w = 0$, and collect the terms that multiply $w$:

$$
(X^\top X + \lambda I)\, w = X^\top y
$$

Read it as: the least-squares equations, with $\lambda$ added to the diagonal. That addition is why the system is always solvable. $X^\top X$ can be singular, but its eigenvalues are never negative, so adding $\lambda$ to the diagonal makes every eigenvalue at least $\lambda > 0$, and a matrix with no zero eigenvalue has an inverse. On the one-weight example, $X^\top X = 0.01$ and $X^\top y = 0.1$, which is the equation you found above. On the identity example, $(I + I)\,w = (3, 1)$ gives $w = (1.5, 0.5)$, the better score from a moment ago.

```python
d = X.shape[1]
w = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(d), X.T @ y)   # no np.linalg.inv
```

The fee also repairs copied columns. Take $X$ with rows $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$, and labels $(1, 2)$. Any weights with $w_1 + w_2 = 1$ fit exactly, so least squares has infinitely many answers. With $\lambda = 1$ the system is $\begin{bmatrix} 6 & 5 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} w = (5, 5)$, with the single answer $w = (5/11, 5/11) \approx (0.4545, 0.4545)$. The fee breaks the tie by preferring the pair of weights with the smallest squared length, which by symmetry is the even split.

> [!KEY]
> A model leans on large weights when the data cannot tell signal from noise. Ridge adds half of lambda times the squared weights to the score, so the fit must pay for every bit of lean. The score is still a smooth bowl, so its bottom is where $X^\top(Xw - y) + \lambda w = 0$, which is one linear system.

## Strong and weak directions

Each solve above is for one value of lambda. To see what changes between values, use a small matrix whose columns are nearly copies of each other, with rows $(3, 3)$ and $(-0.1, 0.1)$, and labels $y = (6, 0.4)$. Least squares gives $w = (-1, 3)$: two large weights with opposite signs, the usual symptom of near-copied columns.

The SVD makes sense of this. The first direction is $v_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$, where both weights move together. It reaches predictions of length $s_1 = 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.24$. The second is $v_2 = (-1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$, where the weights move against each other and the two near-copied columns cancel. It reaches only $s_2 = 0.1\sqrt{2} \approx 0.14$. For this matrix $U$ is the identity, so $u_1 = (1, 0)$ and $u_2 = (0, 1)$. Your numpy may flip the sign of both vectors in a pair, and the product is the same. Figure 2 shows the two reaches.

### Each direction is its own one-weight problem

Describe the weights by how far they go along each direction, $a_i = v_i^\top w$, and the labels by how much of them lies along each $u_i$, $c_i = u_i^\top y$. Here $c = (6, 0.4)$. Because $U$ and $V$ are rotations, they do not change lengths, so the squared misses and the squared length of the weights both split into one term per direction. (A part of $y$ outside every $u_i$ adds the same constant to every score and drops out.) The ridge score becomes

$$
\sum_i \tfrac12 (s_i a_i - c_i)^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\, a_i^2
$$

Each term involves only its own $a_i$, so you can minimise each one alone. Each is the one-weight problem from the two-point example, with input strength $s_i$ and label $c_i$, and its answer is $s_i c_i / (s_i^2 + \lambda)$.

Take $\lambda = 1$. For the strong direction, $4.24 \times 6 / (18 + 1) = 1.34$, against $6 / 4.24 = 1.41$ from least squares, a small change. For the weak direction, $0.14 \times 0.4 / (0.02 + 1) = 0.055$, against $0.4 / 0.14 = 2.83$ from least squares, a drop of 98%. Rotate back with $w = V a$, which gives $w = (1.34 - 0.055,\; 1.34 + 0.055)/\sqrt{2} = (0.908, 0.987)$. One solve of $(X^\top X + I)\, w = X^\top y$ gives the same. Move the second label to 0.5 and the ridge weights go from $(0.908, 0.987)$ to $(0.898, 0.996)$: a jump of 0.01, where least squares jumped 0.5.

### Read the multiplier aloud

In symbols, the whole path is

$$
w(\lambda) = V\, \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{s_i}{s_i^2 + \lambda}\right) U^\top y
$$

Read it as: rotate the labels into the data's directions with $U^\top$, multiply direction $i$ by $s_i / (s_i^2 + \lambda)$, and rotate back into weights with $V$. Least squares is the case $\lambda = 0$, where the multiplier is $1/s_i$. Figure 3 compares the two.

Why does ridge not delete the weak directions? A hard cut needs a threshold you would have to choose, and a direction just above it would count fully while one just below counts for nothing. The multiplier fades out smoothly, so a direction is trusted in proportion to how clearly the data can see it. When $s_i^2$ is much bigger than $\lambda$, the multiplier is close to $1/s_i$ and ridge changes nothing. When $s_i^2$ is much smaller than $\lambda$, it is close to $s_i/\lambda$, which goes to 0 as the direction gets weaker. At $s = 0.14$ and $\lambda = 1$, least squares multiplies by 7.07 and ridge by 0.139.

The expensive part, the SVD and the product $U^\top y$, does not depend on $\lambda$, so do it once. For each $\lambda$ only the multiplier changes, and a row of weights costs about $r \cdot d$ operations, where $r$ is the number of directions, instead of the $d^3$ of a fresh solve.

```python
c = U.T @ y                              # shape (r,), computed once
factors = shrink_factors(s, lambdas)     # shape (m, r): one row per lambda
path = (factors * c) @ Vt                # shape (m, d): row j holds the weights at lambdas[j]
```

### Count the directions the fit still uses

How many knobs is ridge really turning? The fitted values are $Xw = U\,\mathrm{diag}\big(s_i^2/(s_i^2+\lambda)\big)\,U^\top y$, so direction $i$ keeps the fraction

$$
\frac{s_i^2}{s_i^2 + \lambda}
$$

of what least squares would fit there. This share is 1 for a direction ridge leaves alone, 0.5 when $s_i^2 = \lambda$, and near 0 for a direction it has crushed. Add the shares and you get the *degrees of freedom*, written $\mathrm{df}(\lambda)$: the number of directions the fit still uses. For least squares every direction counts fully, so $\mathrm{df}$ equals the rank. On the running example at $\lambda = 1$ the shares are $18/19 = 0.947$ and $0.02/1.02 = 0.0196$, so $\mathrm{df} = 0.967$. Two weights are in the model, but at this fee it behaves like a model with about one knob.

```python
share = s ** 2 / (s ** 2 + lam)          # one number in [0, 1] per direction
df = np.sum(share)
```

Keep the two lookalike formulas apart. The multiplier $s/(s^2 + \lambda)$ turns a label part into a weight and can be far above 1, as 7.07 shows. The share $s^2/(s^2 + \lambda)$ is the multiplier times $s$, and always lies between 0 and 1.

If a column is an exact combination of others, some $s_i$ is exactly 0, and at $\lambda = 0$ both the multiplier and the share become $0/0$. The direction does nothing to the predictions, so the usual choice is the shortest weights, which leave that weight at 0. Return 0 for those entries instead of dividing.

> [!WARNING]
> Floating-point arithmetic rarely returns an exact 0. A singular value that should be 0 usually comes back near $10^{-16}$, and at $\lambda = 0$ its multiplier is near $10^{16}$. Drop singular values smaller than about $10^{-12}$ times the largest before you use $\lambda = 0$.

> [!KEY]
> The SVD splits ridge into independent directions, each a one-weight problem with answer $s/(s^2+\lambda)$ times the label part. You pay for one decomposition and get every $\lambda$ for a rescaling of $r$ numbers. Strong directions pass through nearly unchanged and weak ones, where noise hides, are crushed. Adding the shares $s^2/(s^2+\lambda)$ counts how many directions the fit still uses.

> [!RECAP]
> - Ridge scores a fit as half the squared misses plus half of lambda times the squared weights. On the identity example, $(1.5, 0.5)$ scores 2.5 and $(3, 1)$ scores 5.
> - The gradient is $X^\top(Xw - y) + \lambda w$, and setting it to zero gives $(X^\top X + \lambda I)\, w = X^\top y$, solved with `np.linalg.solve`.
> - The SVD splits the problem into directions. Direction $i$ has weight $s_i c_i / (s_i^2 + \lambda)$, and $\lambda = 1$ turns weights $(-1, 3)$ into $(0.908, 0.987)$.
> - Compute $U^\top y$ once. Each $\lambda$ then only changes the multiplier.
> - The degrees of freedom add the shares $s_i^2 / (s_i^2 + \lambda)$, and a zero singular value gives $0/0$ at $\lambda = 0$, so return 0 for that entry.

## Build it step by step

You write six functions, in the order the checks test them.

1. **`ridge_objective(X, y, w, lam)`** returns a float. Compute the misses `X @ w - y`, then half their squared sum plus `0.5 * lam * np.dot(w, w)`. The most common mistake is to drop the half on the fee, which turns 2.5 into 3.75 on the identity example.
2. **`ridge_gradient(X, y, w, lam)`** returns an array of shape `(d,)`. It is `X.T @ misses + lam * w`. A common mistake is `lam * w.sum()`, a single number, where each weight needs its own pull. The checks compare your answer with finite differences of `ridge_objective`.
3. **`ridge_closed_form(X, y, lam)`** returns an array of shape `(d,)`. Build `X.T @ X + lam * np.eye(d)` and hand it to `np.linalg.solve` with `X.T @ y`. Calling `np.linalg.inv` does more work and keeps fewer digits for the same answer.
4. **`shrink_factors(s, lambdas)`** takes `s` of shape `(r,)` and `lambdas` of shape `(m,)`, and returns an array of shape `(m, r)`. Build `s[None, :] ** 2 + lambdas[:, None]` as the denominator and divide `s` by it, returning 0 where the denominator is exactly 0. Using `lambdas[None, :]` gives the shape `(r, m)`, or an error.
5. **`ridge_svd_path(U, s, Vt, y, lambdas)`** returns an array of shape `(m, d)`. Compute `U.T @ y` once, multiply it by the factors, and finish with `@ Vt`. Skipping `@ Vt` leaves the directional weights $a_i$ rather than the weights $w$.
6. **`effective_degrees_of_freedom(s, lam)`** returns a float when `lam` is a number, and an array of shape `(m,)` when it is an array. Sum the shares $s^2/(s^2+\lambda)$ over the last axis, so that each lambda gets one total. Using $s/(s^2+\lambda)$ gives 0.099 instead of 0.0099 at $s = 0.1$ and $\lambda = 1$.

## Cost and scaling

For $n$ rows and $d$ columns, one SVD costs a few times $nd^2$. After that each $\lambda$ costs about $r \cdot d$ operations, with $r \le d$, while a fresh solve per lambda costs about $d^3$ each. With 50 lambdas the SVD route wins by a wide margin once $d$ is in the hundreds, and the SVD is also the more accurate way to solve.

## Where this breaks

- **Columns on different scales.** The fee charges every weight in the same currency, so a column measured in tiny units needs a huge weight and is punished for it. Divide each column by its spread before you fit.
- **A fee on the intercept.** A constant offset in the labels should not be shrunk, because shifting every label by the same amount would then change the fit. Subtract the mean from $y$ and from each column first, or leave the bias out of the fee.
- **Choosing lambda.** Too small and the fit still chases noise. Too large and every weight collapses towards 0. Fit on training data for a grid of lambdas such as 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10, score each fit on held-out data, and keep the lambda that scores best.

The ridge dual, which leads to kernel ridge, is in [convex duality](/lessons/019-convex-duality).
