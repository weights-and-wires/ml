# Loss from size and data

You have a language model with 1 billion parameters, trained on 2 billion tokens, and its loss is 2.795. You have money for a bigger model, so you double its size. The loss moves to 2.722, an improvement of 2.6 percent. A colleague asks why you did not double the training text instead. Doing that on the original model gives 2.636, more than twice the improvement, and it costs about the same extra compute.

The loss was never one number that falls as you spend more. It has more than one cause, and spending on the wrong cause barely moves it. Without a way to tell which cause is biggest, you guess, and an expensive guess can leave the loss almost where it was.

This chapter splits the loss into three pieces: a floor that nothing removes, a penalty for having too few parameters, and a penalty for having seen too few tokens. Once you can compute each piece, "what should I grow?" becomes a question with an answer.

## What you already know

- A power law $c\,x^{k}$ multiplies its output by $2^{k}$ whenever $x$ doubles, and a log-log line fits its exponent.
- A parameter is one learned number in the model, and a token is one piece of text the model reads, so "1 billion parameters on 2 billion tokens" means a billion numbers fitted against two billion pieces of text.
- A language model's loss is the average of $-\ln p$ over the true next tokens, so a lower loss means the model was less surprised.
- The exponential of that loss is the perplexity, the number of equally likely choices the model is torn between.
- A fit is only a description of measurements, and its constants have to be measured.

Figure 1 fixes the training text at 20 billion tokens and grows the model from 10 million parameters to a trillion. The loss falls quickly at first. Then the curve flattens out, and it flattens along the upper dashed line at 2.036, not along the lower one at 1.69. No model size, however large, gets below it on this much text.

The lower dashed line at 1.69 is the floor, written $E$. Text is partly unpredictable, since many words can follow "the" and the model cannot know which one the author chose, so some loss is left even for a perfect model. In perplexity terms, $e^{1.69} \approx 5.4$ equally likely choices remain for a perfect model, and the loss of 2.384 at 1 billion parameters on 20 billion tokens is a perplexity of about 10.8. The gap between 2.036 and 1.69 is what the training text could not teach, however large the model.

## Two bottlenecks that add

Think of a student preparing for an exam with a notebook and a pile of pages to read. If the notebook is tiny, the student cannot hold what they learn, no matter how many pages they read. If the student has read two pages, a huge notebook is mostly blank, so it cannot help either. Each problem has its own cause and its own cure. A bigger notebook fixes only the first, and more pages fix only the second.

A model has the same two limits. **Too few parameters** means it cannot store the patterns, so it must blur them. **Too few tokens** means it has not seen enough examples to learn the patterns it could store. The loss then has a floor plus one penalty for each limit:

$$
L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}
$$

Read it aloud: the loss is the floor, plus a size penalty that is a constant $A$ divided by the number of parameters $N$ raised to the power $\alpha$ (alpha, a small positive number), plus a data penalty that is a constant $B$ divided by the number of tokens $D$ raised to the power $\beta$ (beta). Each penalty is a power law from the last chapter, with a negative exponent, and each shrinks toward zero as its own input grows. They add because they are separate causes, and each one depends on only one variable. This additive form is a fitted description that matches experiments well, not something derived from first principles.

## A worked example

Use $E = 1.69$, $A = 400$, $B = 1100$, and $\alpha = \beta = 0.34$. The constants are stylised round numbers so you can check the arithmetic, and they are close to the published fit in spirit only: the published data exponent is smaller, about 0.28. Take $N = 10^9$ and $D = 2 \times 10^{10}$.

The size penalty is $400 / (10^9)^{0.34} = 400 / 1148.2 = 0.3484$. The data penalty is $1100 / (2 \times 10^{10})^{0.34} = 1100 / 3179.4 = 0.3460$. The loss is $1.69 + 0.3484 + 0.3460 = 2.3844$. Here the two penalties are nearly equal, so neither side is the bottleneck. In numpy the order of the constants matters:

```python
E, A, B, alpha, beta = coeffs
size_penalty = A / N**alpha
data_penalty = B / D**beta
```

Return to the opening, with $D = 2 \times 10^{9}$ instead. The data penalty is $1100 / (2 \times 10^{9})^{0.34} = 0.7569$, and the loss is $1.69 + 0.3484 + 0.7569 = 2.7953$. The data piece is more than twice the size piece, so data is the bottleneck.

Figure 2 shows what each doubling does. Doubling the model multiplies the size piece by $2^{-0.34} = 0.79$, so it falls from 0.3484 to 0.2752, and the loss falls by 0.073. Doubling the data multiplies the data piece by the same 0.79, from 0.7569 to 0.5979, and the loss falls by 0.159. The percentages are equal and the amounts are not, because the data piece starts out bigger. Whichever penalty is larger has more to give back, which is why the answer to the opening question is to grow the bigger penalty.

One result in Figure 2 is easy to miss. The drop from doubling the model, $A/N^{\alpha}\,(1 - 2^{-\alpha})$, contains no $D$ at all. A bigger model gains 0.073 whether you have 2 billion tokens or 2 trillion. What the data changes is how large that gain looks next to the loss that remains. With a large data penalty, a gain of 0.073 is a small share of the whole.

> [!KEY]
> Loss is a floor plus a size penalty plus a data penalty. Each penalty shrinks as its own input grows and does nothing for the other, so growing only one of them eventually stops helping, and the larger penalty is the one worth shrinking.

## Where the constants come from

Nobody derives $A$ or $\alpha$. They are measured, with the tool from the last chapter. Suppose you train models of many sizes on so much text that the data penalty is nearly constant, and you know the floor. Subtract the floor and that constant from each loss and what is left is $A/N^{\alpha}$, a power law in $N$. A log-log fit has slope $-\alpha$ and coefficient $A$. Slicing the other way, with the model huge and the data varied, gives $B$ and $\beta$.

Real experiments cannot make the model or the data infinite and do not know $E$ ahead of time, so people fit all five constants at once to a grid of runs with a general optimiser. The slice view is still the right way to read the constants. $\alpha$ says how fast the size penalty shrinks per doubling. $A$ is the penalty at one parameter, a scale that only means something next to $B$.

> [!RECAP]
> - The loss is $E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}$, with constants in the order `(E, A, B, alpha, beta)`.
> - $E$ is the floor no model removes. The size penalty falls with parameters and the data penalty falls with tokens.
> - Doubling $N$ multiplies the size penalty by $2^{-\alpha}$ and leaves the data penalty alone, so the gain does not depend on $D$.
> - The larger penalty is the bottleneck, because it has more loss left to give back.
> - The constants come from power-law fits, and the ones in this chapter are stylised.

## Build it step by step

You write three functions, and the checks call them in this order. `fit_power_law` from the last chapter is already in the starter file, and the `__main__` block uses it to recover $\alpha$ and $A$ from a slice.

1. `loss_terms(N, D, coeffs)` takes the sizes and the tuple `coeffs`, and returns the tuple `(floor, size_penalty, data_penalty)`. The most common mistake is to unpack `coeffs` in another order. With $N = 10^9$ and $D = 2 \times 10^{10}$ you should get roughly 1.69, 0.3484, and 0.3460.
2. `predict_loss(N, D, coeffs)` returns the total as one float. It is the sum of the three terms, and the check for 2.3844 is the quickest way to find an unpacking mistake.
3. `marginal_gain(N, D, coeffs, factor=2.0)` returns the loss before growing the model by `factor` minus the loss after. The number is positive when the loss falls. It reuses `predict_loss`, and the sign is the usual slip.

> [!TIP]
> To decide what to grow, compare the two penalties: `"size" if size_penalty > data_penalty else "data"`. At $N = 10^9$ and $D = 2 \times 10^9$ the answer is data, and at $D = 2 \times 10^{10}$ the two are nearly tied.

## Where this breaks

The constants are stylised, so predictions in this chapter are illustrations, not forecasts. Another data mix, tokeniser, or architecture moves all five. The law also predicts loss on text like the training text, and a loss that falls smoothly can hide a task score that jumps.

A check on a real-sized model shows what the law is for. Take 175 billion parameters on 300 billion tokens. With these constants the size penalty is 0.060 and the data penalty is 0.138, more than twice as large. That model is data-starved, which is the claim the Chinchilla study (a 2022 paper from the DeepMind lab, named after the model it trained) made about the largest models of its time: for the compute they used, they were too large and had read too little. The next chapters work out what a balanced split would be.
