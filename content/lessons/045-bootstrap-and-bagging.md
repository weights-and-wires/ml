# Bootstrap samples and bagging

The previous chapter, "Why averaging models cuts noise, and where it stops", showed that averaging a hundred noisy trees beats trusting one. It had a catch that is easy to miss. It averaged trees that each came from their own training set. You have exactly one dataset of 100 rows, and you cannot go back to the market for another 100. How do you get different training sets out of one, without throwing data away?

The obvious fix is to train the same tree a hundred times and average. That does nothing, because a tree is a fixed recipe: the same rows always give the same tree. A hundred identical trees have correlation $\rho = 1$, and the formula from the previous chapter says the average has the variance of one tree. Averaging only helps when the models differ, and models only differ when their training sets do.

This chapter gives a recipe for many different training sets from one dataset, called the bootstrap. It also builds the ensemble you get from it, called bagging.

## What you already know

You already use these ideas:

- A model's variance is how much its prediction moves when its training set is redrawn, and an average of $B$ models has variance $\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/B$ ("Why averaging models cuts noise, and where it stops").
- A stump is a tree with one split and two leaves, and each leaf predicts the mean of its training targets.
- A tree trained on more rows is steadier than a tree trained on fewer rows.
- A random number generator with a seed repeats the same draws.
- A probability of "not" is one minus the probability of "yes", and independent chances multiply.

## Why not just split the data, or shuffle it?

Two ideas come to mind first, and both fail for a reason worth knowing.

You could cut the 100 rows into 10 chunks of 10 and train one tree per chunk. The trees differ, because their rows differ. But each tree sees only 10 rows, and a tree trained on 10 rows is a much noisier model than one trained on 100. The $\sigma^2$ of each model goes up, and the average pays for it.

You could shuffle the 100 rows and train on the shuffled copy. Now every tree sees all 100 rows, but a tree does not care about row order. It looks for the best split in the whole set, so the shuffled copy gives the identical tree. Different order is not a different dataset.

What you need is a copy with the same number of rows that really contains different rows. Sampling with replacement does that.

Look at the stacks and the gaps. Because each ticket goes back in the hat, a row can be drawn two or three times. Because only $n$ draws are made, other rows never come up at all. A tree trained on a draw sees some rows twice and some not at all, so it is a different tree from one trained on another draw. This recipe is the **bootstrap**, and one draw is a **bootstrap sample**. The rows a draw contains are **in the bag**, and the rows it misses are **out of the bag**.

Here is the smallest case. With $n = 4$ rows, say the draw is `idx = [0, 0, 2, 3]`. Row 0 appears twice, rows 2 and 3 once each, and row 1 never. Three of the four distinct rows are in the bag, so the in-bag fraction is $3/4$, and row 1 is out of the bag. In numpy, one draw is one line:

```python
idx = rng.integers(0, n, size=n)
```

The usual mistake is `rng.choice(n, n, replace=False)`. That is the shuffle from the last section: every row appears exactly once, so every tree is the same.

## How many rows does a draw contain?

A given row is picked by any one draw with chance $1/n$, so it is missed with chance $1 - 1/n$. The $n$ draws are independent, so the chance that a row is missed by all of them is that number multiplied $n$ times:

$$
\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^n .
$$

Read it aloud: one draw's miss chance, raised to the number of draws. The table below shows how it behaves.

| $n$ | $(1 - 1/n)^n$ |
|---|---|
| 4 | 0.316 |
| 10 | 0.349 |
| 100 | 0.366 |
| 1000 | 0.368 |

The chance settles near $0.368$, which is $1/e$, and Figure 2 shows how quickly. At $n = 4$ it is $(3/4)^4 = 0.316$, so your draw `[0, 0, 2, 3]` missing one row out of four is an ordinary outcome.

```python
in_bag_fraction = len(np.unique(idx)) / n
```

> [!KEY]
> A bootstrap sample has as many rows as the dataset, but only about 63.2% of the distinct rows. The repeats and the gaps are what make each sample, and so each tree, different.

## Bagging: train on every draw, then average

**Bagging** is short for bootstrap aggregating. Draw $B$ bootstrap samples, train one model on each, and predict with the average of the $B$ models. This is the plan from the previous chapter, with the bootstrap supplying the different training sets.

The trees are still correlated, because every bag comes from the same 100 rows and two bags share most of their rows. So $\rho$ is above zero and the floor $\rho\sigma^2$ is real. Here is a measurement. Grow trees three levels deep on eight noisy points from the sine market of the previous chapter, and look at the prediction at $x = 0.5$ over 2000 redrawn datasets. One tree has variance about $0.26$. The average of 25 bagged trees has variance about $0.15$. Solving the variance-of-an-average formula for $\rho$ gives about $0.58$. Bagging cut the variance by about 40%, and the shared part of the noise is what remains.

A caution about the code you write. The exercise bags stumps, because a stump is short enough to implement, and stumps are the wrong thing to bag for gains. A stump has one split, so it is stiff and its error is mostly bias, not variance, and averaging cannot fix bias. Figure 3 shows the result: the average of 15 stumps is a smoother staircase than one stump, and its squared distance from the true curve is $0.119$ against $0.130$ for a single stump. The method pays off with deep trees, which are the low-bias, high-variance models. The code for them is the same, with a deeper tree in place of `_fit_stump`.

> [!RECAP]
> - Different trees need different training sets, and splitting or shuffling the rows does not give them.
> - A bootstrap sample draws $n$ rows with replacement, so some rows repeat and some are out of the bag.
> - A row is missed with chance $(1-1/n)^n \to 1/e \approx 0.368$, so about 63.2% of the distinct rows are in the bag.
> - Bagging trains one model per bootstrap sample and averages them, which cuts variance down to a floor set by how much the samples overlap.
> - Stumps have mostly bias, so bagging them helps little. Deep trees are the real target.

## Where this breaks

- The bootstrap creates no new information. It reshuffles the rows you already have, so the average cannot be better than what 100 rows can support. An outlier row sits in about 63% of the bags, so it can pull many trees the same way.
- Bags of one dataset always overlap, because they all come from the same $n$ rows. So $\rho$ stays above zero, and the variance stops at the floor from the previous chapter.
- Bagging leaves bias alone. If the single model is too stiff, the average is too stiff.
