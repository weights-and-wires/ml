# Exponential families and generalised linear models

Three jobs land on your desk in one week. Tomorrow's temperature is a number on a line. Whether a visitor clicks a button is yes or no. The number of people who walk into a shop between 9 and 10 is a count: 0, 1, 2, or more.

You can already fit the first two. Squared error fits the temperature, and the log loss fits the click. Each came with its own prediction rule, its own loss, and its own gradient, and you derived each one from scratch. The count job looks like it needs a third set. A straight line is the tempting shortcut, and it fails. A line fitted to counts 3, 1, and 0 at hours 0, 1, and 2 predicts −1.67 arrivals at hour 3, and nobody walks into a shop −1.67 times.

This chapter asks whether one recipe can give the right mean and the right spread for all three jobs, with one loss, one gradient, and one fast fitting loop. It can, and the recipe is a single shape with one dial.

## What you already know

- The log loss for a coin is $\operatorname{softplus}(z) - yz$, where softplus is $\log(1 + e^{z})$ (see [From a score to a probability, and how wrong it is](/lessons/008-sigmoid-and-log-loss)).
- The gradient of the log loss is $X^\top(p - y)$, and Newton's method solves $H\delta = g$, where $H$ is the bend of the loss (see [Fitting logistic regression](/lessons/009-fitting-logistic-regression)).
- A slope says how fast a curve rises, and a bend, the second derivative, says how fast the slope itself changes.
- A mean is the probability-weighted average of the outcomes, a variance is the probability-weighted average squared distance from the mean, and all probabilities add up to 1.
- The running example is a shop with rows $(1, 0)$, $(1, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, where the first entry is a bias column and the second is the hour, and counts 1, 1, and 2.

Call the logarithm of the total $A(\eta)$. At $\eta = 0$ it is $\log 3 = 1.0986$. Here is the surprise. The slope of $A$ is the mean. Check it with a small step of 0.1 on each side:

$$
\frac{A(0.1) - A(-0.1)}{0.2} = \frac{1.2019 - 1.0019}{0.2} = 1.000
$$

That is exactly the mean at $\eta = 0$. The reason is short. A tiny nudge of the dial scales the weight of outcome $y$ by about $1 + y \cdot \text{nudge}$, so the total grows by a factor of $1 + (\text{average } y) \cdot \text{nudge}$. The log of that factor is the mean times the nudge.

The bend of $A$ is the variance. The slope is the mean, so ask how fast the mean moves when you nudge the dial. A nudge of size $\delta$ multiplies each probability by about $1 + (y - \mu)\delta$, where $\mu$ is the current mean, so outcomes above the mean gain and outcomes below it lose. The mean moves by $\delta\sum_y p(y)(y - \mu)^2$, which is $\delta$ times the variance. At $\eta = 0$ the check gives $(1.2019 + 1.0019 - 2 \times 1.0986) / 0.01 = 0.666$, and the variance of outcomes 0, 1, 2 with equal probability is $2/3$. A variance is never negative, so $A$ curves upward.

## One formula for all three

The tilt picture has a compact form. Starting weights are called $h(y)$, and the probability of outcome $y$ is

$$
p(y \mid \eta) = h(y)\, \exp\bigl(\eta\, y - A(\eta)\bigr), \qquad A(\eta) = \log \sum_{y} h(y)\, e^{\eta y}
$$

Read it aloud. The probability is a starting weight $h(y)$, tilted by $e^{\eta y}$, and divided by the total $e^{A(\eta)}$. The division makes the probabilities add up to 1, and it is the only job of $A$, the *log-partition*. The dial $\eta$ is the *natural parameter*, because it sits in the exponent multiplied by $y$.

Because $A$ is the log total, the tilt argument gives both results at once:

$$
\text{mean} = \mu = A'(\eta), \qquad \text{variance} = A''(\eta)
$$

Here $\mu$, pronounced mu, is the mean, and $A'$ and $A''$ are the first and second derivatives. Three families fit this shape:

| Family | Outcomes | $h(y)$ | $A(\eta)$ | Mean $A'(\eta)$ | Variance $A''(\eta)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gaussian, variance 1 | any number | $e^{-y^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ | $\eta^2/2$ | $\eta$ | $1$ |
| Bernoulli | 0 or 1 | $1$ | $\log(1 + e^{\eta})$ | $\sigma(\eta)$ | $\sigma(\eta)(1 - \sigma(\eta))$ |
| Poisson | 0, 1, 2, … | $1/y!$ | $e^{\eta}$ | $e^{\eta}$ | $e^{\eta}$ |

You can verify two rows in a line. The Bernoulli weights are 1 for $y = 0$ and $e^{\eta}$ for $y = 1$, so the total is $1 + e^{\eta}$. The Poisson total is $\sum_y e^{\eta y} / y! = \exp(e^{\eta})$, so $A = e^{\eta}$. For a Gaussian the sum becomes an integral, and the result is the bell curve centred at $\eta$.

Look at the Poisson row. Its mean and its variance are both $e^{\eta}$. A count averaging 100 has a typical wobble of 10, and one averaging 2 has a wobble of about 1.4, so a busy hour varies by more than a quiet one.

> [!KEY]
> Every family here is a tilt of starting weights, divided by a total. The log of that total, $A(\eta)$, has the mean as its slope and the variance as its bend. Only $A$ changes from family to family.

## Let a line drive the dial

Why not set the mean to a linear score directly? A mean lives in a restricted range, a probability between 0 and 1 or a count above 0, and a straight line leaves it, which is the −1.67 arrivals from the start of the chapter. Any real number is a valid dial setting, so let a line choose $\eta$, and let $A'$ map the dial into the range the mean must stay in:

$$
\eta = Xw, \qquad \mu = A'(\eta)
$$

Here $X$ is the table of features with one row per example, $w$ holds the weights, and $\mu$ is the vector of predicted means. A model built this way is a *generalised linear model*, or GLM: linear in the dial, then bent by $A'$. For the Poisson, $\mu = e^{\eta}$, which is positive for every $\eta$, so no weights can produce a negative count. For a coin, $A'$ is the sigmoid, and you recover logistic regression.

The reverse map, from a mean back to a dial setting, is the *link*. It exists because a variance is positive, so $A'$ always rises with the dial. A model that uses exactly this reverse map has the *canonical* link: the identity for the Gaussian, the logit for the Bernoulli, and the log for the Poisson. You never code the link here, because you only go from dial to mean.

## Score the model by how surprised it is

Score each row by the probability the model gave to what happened, and take the negative log so that bigger means worse. From the shared formula, the negative log of the probability of label $y$ at dial $\eta$ is

$$
-\log p(y \mid \eta) = A(\eta) - y\,\eta - \log h(y)
$$

Rows are independent, so the total surprise is the sum over rows. The last term, $\log h(y)$, depends only on the label, so it cannot change which weights are best. Drop it, and the loss is

$$
L(w) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[A(\eta_i) - y_i\,\eta_i\bigr], \qquad \eta = Xw
$$

Read it aloud: for every row, add the log-partition of its dial reading and subtract the label times that reading. Each family turns it into a loss you know.

| Family | $A(\eta) - y\eta$ | Which loss is it |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian | $\tfrac12\eta^2 - y\eta = \tfrac12(\eta - y)^2 - \tfrac12 y^2$ | Half the squared error, plus a constant |
| Bernoulli | $\operatorname{softplus}(\eta) - y\eta$ | The log loss from [the earlier chapter](/lessons/008-sigmoid-and-log-loss) |
| Poisson | $e^{\eta} - y\eta$ | Punishes a dial that is too high exponentially, and one that is too low only linearly |

One loss covers all three, and squared error is its Gaussian case. At $w = (0, 0)$ the shop rows each score $A(0) - y \cdot 0 = e^{0} = 1$, so the loss is $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$.

> [!WARNING]
> This loss drops $\log h(y)$, so it is not the true negative log-likelihood. For the Poisson the true value is this loss plus $\sum_i \log y_i!$, which is $3 + \log 2 = 3.693$ on the shop at zero weights. The shift does not move the best weights.

## The gradient is prediction minus truth

Differentiate one row. The weights enter only through $\eta_i = x_i \cdot w$, so $\partial \eta_i / \partial w = x_i$. The first piece gives $\partial A(\eta_i)/\partial w = A'(\eta_i)\, x_i = \mu_i\, x_i$, because the slope of $A$ is the mean. The second piece gives $\partial (y_i \eta_i)/\partial w = y_i\, x_i$. Subtract:

$$
\nabla L(w) = \sum_i (\mu_i - y_i)\, x_i = X^{\top}(\mu - y)
$$

Read it aloud: each weight is pushed by the prediction minus the truth, multiplied by how large its feature was. This is the same shape as the gradient you used for coins, and it holds for every family because the slope of $A$ is the mean. In numpy:

```python
mu = mean_from_natural(family, X @ w)
grad = X.T @ (mu - y)   # shape (d,)
```

Check it on the shop. The residuals $\mu - y$ at zero weights are $0$, $0$, and $-1$. The first weight collects their sum, $-1$. The second weights each by the hour, so it gets $-2$. The gradient is $[-1, -2]$.

> [!WARNING]
> Writing $y - \mu$ flips the gradient to $[1, 2]$, and every step then walks uphill.

Every fit has one bottom. The loss for a row is $A(\eta_i)$ minus a term that is a straight line in the weights. A line does not bend and $A$ bends upward, so the sum is a bowl with no false dips. Two conditions keep the bottom unique: the columns of $X$ must carry independent information, and coin labels must not split perfectly, because then the loss keeps falling as the weights grow.

Take small steps against the gradient, $w \leftarrow w - \text{lr} \cdot \nabla L(w)$. From zero weights on the shop, one step of 0.05 gives $w = [0.05, 0.1]$ and the loss falls from 3 to 2.797. After 500 steps the weights reach $[-0.1451, 0.3842]$ with loss 2.65951, the bottom of the bowl. At the bottom the gradient is 0, which means the residuals $\mu - y$, here $-0.135$, $+0.270$, and $-0.135$, sum to 0 and also sum to 0 when weighted by the hour. The predictions add up to 4, the same as the counts.

> [!KEY]
> Setting the dial to a linear score turns a family into a model. Its loss is $A(\eta) - y\eta$, the negative log of the probability of the label with the constant dropped, and its gradient is $X^\top(\mu - y)$ for every family. Squared error and the log loss are two cases of one loss.

## Let the bend set the step

Plain descent got there, but it needed a step size picked by hand and about 100 steps to reach the bottom to four digits. [Newton's method](/lessons/009-fitting-logistic-regression) divides the slope by the bend instead, so where does the bend come from in every family?

Each row contributes $A(\eta_i) - y_i\eta_i$ to the loss. The second piece is a straight line in $w$, so it has no bend. The first piece bends in the dial by $A''(\eta_i)$, the variance of that row, and the dial is $\eta_i = x_i \cdot w$, so the bend in the weights is $A''(\eta_i)\,x_i x_i^\top$. Add up the rows:

$$
H(w) = X^\top D X, \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(A''(\eta_1), \ldots, A''(\eta_n)\bigr)
$$

Read it aloud. $D$ holds one variance per row, and $X^\top D X$ weights each row's feature product by that variance. The label $y$ appears nowhere in it.

Why variance? It is how fast a row's predicted mean moves when the dial moves. A near-certain coin barely changes its prediction, so it tells you little about the weights and gets a small weight in $D$: a coin at $p = 0.5$ has variance 0.25, and one at $p = 0.95$ has 0.0475, about a fifth as much. A busy Poisson hour changes its mean quickly, so it gets a large one. In numpy:

```python
info = (X * var[:, None]).T @ X     # shape (d, d)
```

At $w = 0$ on the shop, every Poisson variance is $e^{0} = 1$, so $D$ is the identity and $H = X^\top X$. The columns $(1,1,1)$ and $(0,1,2)$ have dot products $3$, $3$, $3$, and $0 + 1 + 4 = 5$:

$$
H = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad g = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Solve $H\delta = g$. The equations are $3\delta_1 + 3\delta_2 = -1$ and $3\delta_1 + 5\delta_2 = -2$. Their difference is $2\delta_2 = -1$, so $\delta_2 = -0.5$, and then $3\delta_1 = -1 + 1.5 = 0.5$, so $\delta_1 = 1/6$. Subtract the step, $w \leftarrow w - \delta$, and the weights are $[-1/6, 0.5]$. The loss falls from 3 to 2.7097. A third step reaches $[-0.1451, 0.3843]$ with loss 2.65951, where gradient descent needed about 100 steps to match the loss to four digits. Figure 4 shows both paths.

The bowl is steep across and gentle along, so one step size must be small enough for the steep direction. The bend table scales each direction separately.

*Fisher information* is the bend of the loss averaged over the labels the model itself might produce. The bend $X^\top D X$ contains no label, so averaging changes nothing and the Fisher information is the true bend. The update

$$
w \leftarrow w - I(w)^{-1} \nabla L(w), \qquad I(w) = X^\top D X
$$

is called *Fisher scoring*, and with the canonical link it is Newton's method exactly. With another link between dial and mean, such as the probit for coins, the bend does contain the labels, and Fisher scoring becomes a different method from Newton.

Two more cases confirm it. For coins at zero weights on rows $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 2)$ with labels 1 and 0, every variance is 0.25, so $g = [0, 1]$, $H = [[0.5, 0.5], [0.5, 1]]$, and $\delta = [-2, 2]$, the Newton step from [the last chapter](/lessons/009-fitting-logistic-regression). For the Gaussian, every variance is 1 and the bend $X^\top X$ never changes, because the loss is a perfect parabola. On the same rows with labels 3 and 1, $g = [-4, -2]$, $H = [[2, 2], [2, 4]]$, and $\delta = [-3, 1]$, so the weights become $[3, -1]$, the least-squares answer, in one step.

> [!KEY]
> The loss bends by $X^\top D X$, where $D$ holds each row's variance, and no label appears in it. Newton's step with that table is Fisher scoring. It sets its own step length from the bowl's shape, and for every family it is the same loop: a Poisson fit costs the same as a logistic fit of the same shape.

> [!RECAP]
> - The dial $\eta$ tilts starting weights, and $A(\eta)$ is the log of their total. The mean is $A'(\eta)$ and the variance is $A''(\eta)$.
> - $A$ is $\eta^2/2$, softplus, or $e^{\eta}$ for the Gaussian, Bernoulli, and Poisson.
> - The dial is $\eta = Xw$ and the prediction is $\mu = A'(\eta)$, which keeps counts positive and probabilities inside $(0, 1)$.
> - The loss is $\sum[A(\eta) - y\eta]$ and the gradient is $X^\top(\mu - y)$. On the shop at zero weights they are 3 and $[-1, -2]$.
> - The bend is $X^\top D X$ with one variance per row, and the update is $w \leftarrow w - \delta$ with $H\delta = g$.

## Build it step by step

Every function takes a family name, one of `"gaussian"`, `"bernoulli"`, or `"poisson"`, and raises `ValueError` for any other name. Build eight functions, in this order:

1. `log_partition(family, eta)` returns $A(\eta)$ with the shape of `eta`: `0.5 * eta ** 2`, the stable softplus, or `np.exp(eta)`. The common mistake is the naive softplus, which overflows at large `eta`.

2. `mean_from_natural(family, eta)` returns $A'(\eta)$. For the Bernoulli, `np.exp(-np.logaddexp(0, -eta))` is a sigmoid that stays finite on either side. The common mistake is `1 / (1 + np.exp(-eta))`, which overflows.

3. `variance_from_natural(family, eta)` returns $A''(\eta)$. Use `np.ones_like(eta)`, `p * (1 - p)` from the mean you wrote earlier, or `np.exp(eta)`. The common mistake is returning the bare number `1` for the Gaussian, which has the wrong shape.

4. `glm_loss(family, w, X, y)` returns one number. Compute `eta = X @ w` and return `np.sum(log_partition(family, eta) - y * eta)`. The common mistake is averaging instead of summing, which changes the scale the checks expect.

5. `glm_gradient(family, w, X, y)` returns an array of shape `(d,)`. Compute `mu` from `eta`, then return `X.T @ (mu - y)`. The common mistake is the sign, `y - mu`.

6. `glm_fisher_information(family, w, X)` returns a `(d, d)` array. Compute the dial `X @ w`, get the variances, and return `(X * var[:, None]).T @ X`. The common mistake is `np.diag(var)`, which builds an $n \times n$ table.

7. `newton_step(family, w, X, y)` returns the step $\delta$, not the updated weights, as `np.linalg.solve(info, grad)`. The common mistake is `np.linalg.inv(info) @ grad`.

8. `fit_fisher_scoring(family, X, y, max_iter, tol)` starts at zero weights and returns `(w, history)`. In each pass, append the loss, stop if the gradient norm is below `tol`, and otherwise subtract the step. The common mistake is recording the loss after the step, so index 0 is not the loss at zero weights.

## Where this breaks

- **Counts that spread wider than their mean.** Real arrival counts often vary more than a Poisson allows. This is *overdispersion*, and a model that assumes the Poisson tie reports more confidence than the data deserve.
- **A perfect split of coin labels.** The loss has no bottom, the variances shrink toward 0, and the bend table becomes nearly singular. A ridge fee, as in [Ridge regression](/lessons/006-ridge-regression), keeps it solvable. This exercise has no ridge.
- **A start far from the bottom.** A step from far away can overshoot and raise the loss, and $e^{\eta}$ overflows past about 709. Halving the step until the loss falls fixes the first problem.
- **Other links and other distributions.** With a non-canonical link the bend contains the labels, so the short formula for it is wrong. A Cauchy bump has no mean at all, so it has no $A$.

## The exercise

You implement `log_partition`, `mean_from_natural`, `variance_from_natural`, `glm_loss`, `glm_gradient`, `glm_fisher_information`, `newton_step`, and `fit_fisher_scoring`. The checks compare the three family functions with $\eta^2/2$, softplus, and $e^{\eta}$ (including at $\eta = \pm 800$) and with finite-difference slopes and bends, and confirm that the probabilities add up to 1. They confirm that `glm_loss` is 3 on the shop data at zero weights, that `glm_gradient` is $[-1, -2]$ and matches finite differences, and that the information table is $[[3, 3], [3, 5]]$. Last, they check that `newton_step` returns $[1/6, -0.5]$, $[-2, 2]$, and $[-3, 1]$ on the shop, coin, and Gaussian rows, and that `fit_fisher_scoring` recovers known Poisson weights in under ten iterations and solves the Gaussian case in one step.
