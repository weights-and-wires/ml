# Fitting a curve, and the two ways it fails: degree, bias, and variance

In the made-up market of the [previous chapter](/lessons/001-loss-and-honest-scores), rent rises, falls, and rises again as size grows, so a straight line misses the bends. The memorising rule is the opposite problem. It follows every wobble in the rents, including the landlord's mood, and fails on flats it has not seen.

You want something in between: a curve that bends enough to follow the real pattern and not so much that it chases the wobble. Is there one dial that goes from stiff to wiggly? If there is, where on the dial is the best rule? And does a stiff rule fail in the same way as a wiggly one?

This chapter builds the dial from polynomials, turns it while you watch the training score and the held-out score, and then splits the error into three named parts. Two of them you can reduce, and one you cannot. The split tells you which way to turn the dial, and what the best possible score is.

## What you already know

You already use these ideas:

- Empirical risk is the average loss, and squared loss is the squared miss.
- A training score is too kind, so a rule is judged on flats it was not fit to.
- A power of a number multiplies it by itself: $2^3=2\times2\times2=8$, and any number to the power 0 is 1.
- A weighted sum such as `1*1 + 3*2 + 0.5*4` multiplies each value by a weight and adds the results. numpy writes a weighted sum of many rows as `phi @ w`.
- A variance is the average squared distance of numbers from their own average.

## One dial, many shapes

The data are the made-up market from the previous chapter. Size runs from 0 to 1, rent is measured in hundreds of pounds above or below typical, and this chapter fits 15 flats.

A *polynomial* of degree $p$ builds a guess from powers of the size $x$:

$$
\hat{y}=w_0+w_1x+w_2x^2+\dots+w_px^p
$$

Read it aloud: the guess is a constant $w_0$, plus $w_1$ times the size, plus $w_2$ times the size squared, and so on up to the $p$-th power. The numbers $w_0,\dots,w_p$ are the *weights*. They are the knobs. Degree 1 is a straight line, degree 2 allows one bend, and every extra degree allows more bends. Degree is the dial, and the weights are what you fit once the dial is set.

Take a tiny case. Let $p=2$ with weights 1, 3, and 0.5, and let the size be $x=2$. The powers are $2^0=1$, $2^1=2$, and $2^2=4$. The guess is $1\times1+3\times2+0.5\times4=1+6+2=9$.

## Turn a curve into a table

How do you find the weights? Look at the formula again. The guess is bent in $x$, but it is only a weighted sum of the numbers $1$, $x$, $x^2$, and so on. Each weight multiplies one of those numbers and nothing else, with no weight inside a power or a product with another weight. If you compute those numbers first and put them in columns, the curve problem becomes a plain weighted-sum problem: find one weight per column. The bend lives in the columns, and the weights stay simple.

Each flat becomes one row of the table, and column $j$ holds $x^j$. For sizes 2 and −1 at degree 2, row one is $1, 2, 4$ and row two is $(-1)^0, (-1)^1, (-1)^2$, which is $1, -1, 1$. This table is the *design matrix*, written $\phi$ (the Greek letter phi, said "fie"). Multiply it by the weights $(1, 3, 0.5)$ and you get one guess per row: 9 for the first flat and $1-3+0.5=-1.5$ for the second. In numpy, one line builds every column:

```python
phi = x[:, None] ** np.arange(degree + 1)[None, :]   # shape (n, degree + 1), column j is x ** j
guesses = phi @ w                                    # shape (n,)
```

Two small traps live here. `np.arange(degree)` stops one power short and gives one column too few. A size of 0 is safe, because numpy computes `0.0 ** 0` as 1, so column 0 stays all ones.

> [!TIP]
> Print `phi.shape` right after you build it. It must read `(n, degree + 1)`.

## Choose the weights: least squares

Which weights? Use the squared-loss score you already know. In Figure 1 each red segment is the vertical miss between a flat and the curve, and the empirical risk adds up their squares. *Least squares* picks the weights that make the sum of the squared vertical misses as small as possible. Dividing the sum by $n$ gives the empirical risk and does not move the minimum, so the two descriptions pick the same weights.

Why not scan, as the previous chapter did for the rent slope? A degree-12 curve has 13 weights, and nine candidates for each would be $9^{13}$, about 2.5 trillion curves to score. You need a solver that goes straight to the best weights.

The chapter on [fitting a line by minimising squared misses](/lessons/003-least-squares-and-projection) derives the solution. Until then, treat numpy's solver as a tool with one clear promise: it returns the weights with the smallest total squared miss. Check the promise on a case you can do by eye. The three points $(0,1)$, $(1,3)$, and $(2,5)$ lie exactly on the line $1+2x$, so the solver must return weights 1 and 2. It does. One more property of the answer, which the exercise also checks: at the least-squares weights, the misses are at right angles to every column of $\phi$, so `phi.T @ misses` is all zeros. That says no small nudge to the weights lowers the total miss.

```python
w = np.linalg.lstsq(phi, y, rcond=None)[0]   # lstsq returns a tuple; item 0 holds the weights
```

The usual mistake is to keep the whole tuple. The weights are the first item only.

The held-out risk falls from 0.438 at degree 1 to 0.105 at degree 3, and then climbs, slowly at first and then explosively. Degree 2 scores the same as degree 1 here (0.168 on the training flats). The wave is balanced about the middle of the size range, so the single bend that degree 2 adds has nothing to bend toward. The dial does not have to move smoothly.

The best degree is the one with the lowest *held-out* risk: here, degree 3. The training risk cannot find it, because the training risk prefers the highest degree every time. > [!KEY]
> A polynomial's degree is a dial from stiff to wiggly. The training risk falls as you turn it up, which hides the damage, and only a held-out score shows where extra wiggle stops helping and starts hurting.

Look at the left panel. The straight lines are all alike. Whichever 15 flats the market hands them, they are stiff, so they bunch together. But the average of the lines, the blue dashed one, sits far from the black wave near the hill at $x=0.25$. This error is the same in every training set. It is *bias*: the part of the miss that comes from the rule being unable to bend to the truth.

Now look at the right panel. The degree-5 curves are centred on the wave, so the average of the fits is close to the truth. But individual curves scatter, because each one is bent by the wobble in its own 15 flats. The width of that fan is the *variance*: how much a fit changes when the training set changes. Bias is about the average of the fits, and variance is about their spread.

The third part is not in the picture. Even a fit that equals the true wave exactly will miss a real flat, because the real rent also contains the landlord's wobble. This is *noise*: the part of the rent that no rule based on size can predict. It is the same for every curve, so it sets a floor on the error.

## Count the three parts by hand

Fix one flat size, $x=0.25$, where the true rent is 1.0. Suppose four fits, each to a different training set, give the guesses 0.8, 1.2, 1.0, and 1.4. Think of darts: each fit throws one dart at the true rent, so the exercise calls the guesses `hits` and the true rent the `bullseye`.

The average guess is 1.1. It sits 0.1 above the truth, so the squared bias is $0.1^2=0.01$. The guesses sit at distances $-0.3$, $0.1$, $-0.1$, and $0.3$ from their average, and their squares are 0.09, 0.01, 0.01, and 0.09. The sum is 0.20, and dividing by the 4 guesses gives a variance of 0.05. The noise has standard deviation 0.3, so its variance is $0.3^2=0.09$.

Add the three parts: $0.01+0.05+0.09=0.15$. Check that this equals the expected squared miss against a fresh rent. A fresh rent is the truth 1.0 plus a wobble that averages 0 and has variance 0.09, so each guess has an expected squared miss of its squared distance from 1.0, plus 0.09. The squared distances of the four guesses from 1.0 are 0.04, 0.04, 0, and 0.16, which average to 0.06. So the expected squared miss is $0.06+0.09=0.15$, the same number.

## Why the three parts add

At a fixed flat size $x$, write $f(x)$ for the true rent, $\hat{f}(x)$ for the guess of one fit, and $\bar{f}(x)$ for the average of $\hat{f}(x)$ over all the training sets you could draw. The symbol $\sigma$ (sigma) is the standard deviation of the wobble, so $\sigma^2$ is its variance. Then

$$
\text{expected miss}^2=\big(\bar{f}(x)-f(x)\big)^2+\operatorname{E}\big[(\hat{f}(x)-\bar{f}(x))^2\big]+\sigma^2
$$

Here $\operatorname{E}[\cdot]$ means the average over fresh training sets and over a fresh rent for this flat. Read it aloud: the expected squared miss is the squared gap between the average fit and the truth, plus the average squared wobble of a fit around its own average, plus the variance of the label noise. Those are bias squared, variance, and noise.

Why does the sum have no extra terms? Split the miss into three pieces: $(\hat{f}-\bar{f})$, then $(\bar{f}-f)$, then $(f-y)$, where $y$ is the fresh rent. Squaring a sum of three pieces also gives cross terms, the products of two different pieces. Each cross term averages to zero. The first piece averages to 0 over training sets and the second is one fixed number, so their product averages to 0. The third piece, the wobble, averages to 0 and is independent of the training set, so products with it vanish too. That leaves the three squares, and the identity is exact, not an approximation.

## Measure the parts by simulation

You cannot average over all possible training sets, but you can average over many. This is a *Monte Carlo* estimate, and its error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{T}$ for $T$ trials, so quadrupling the trials halves the error. Draw a training set, fit the polynomial, and record the guesses at the query sizes. Do this $T$ times and stack the guesses in an array with one row per trial and one column per query size. Then reduce down the trials:

```python
mean_pred = predictions.mean(axis=0)               # (q,): the average fit at each query size
bias_sq = (mean_pred - true_function(x_query)) ** 2
variance = predictions.var(axis=0)                 # divides by the number of trials
noise = np.full(len(x_query), noise_std ** 2)      # needs no trials at all
```

`axis=0` runs down the trials and leaves one number per query size. `axis=1` would run across the query sizes and leave one number per trial, which is the wrong answer.

> [!WARNING]
> Reduce over `axis=0`, with one row per trial and one column per query size. Each result must have shape `(q,)`. Also square the noise: `noise_std ** 2`, not `noise_std`.

> [!KEY]
> At each flat size, the expected squared error is squared bias plus variance plus noise. Stiff rules fail by bias, because they miss the truth in the same way every time. Wiggly rules fail by variance, because their guesses scatter. Noise stays whatever you choose. The rule "rent is ten times the size" from the chapter on [scoring a rule](/lessons/001-loss-and-honest-scores) is stiff, so it fails by bias, and a curve that threads every flat in the folder is wiggly, so it fails by variance. The held-out risk measures the sum, and the best degree is where the falling bias meets the rising variance.

> [!RECAP]
> - A polynomial of degree $p$ has $p+1$ weights. The design matrix has one column per power, shape `(n, p + 1)`, and `np.linalg.lstsq(...)[0]` holds the least-squares weights.
> - Training risk falls with degree. Held-out risk falls and then rises, so choose the degree with the lowest held-out risk.
> - Bias is the gap between the average fit and the truth. Variance is the spread of fits around their own average. Noise is the wobble no rule can predict.
> - At one flat size, the expected squared miss equals bias squared plus variance plus noise variance.
> - Estimate the parts with one row of guesses per training set, and reduce down `axis=0`.

## Build it step by step

The starter file provides `squared_loss` and `empirical_risk` from the previous chapter, and `predict_polynomial`, which depends on your `polynomial_features`. You write six functions, in the order the checks test them.

1. **`polynomial_features(x, degree)`** takes `x` of shape `(n,)` and returns shape `(n, degree + 1)`, where column `j` is `x ** j`:

   ```python
   powers = np.arange(degree + 1)   # 0, 1, ..., degree
   # x[:, None] ** powers[None, :]
   ```

   `np.arange(degree)` leaves out the last power.

2. **`fit_polynomial(x, y, degree)`** returns the least-squares weights, shape `(degree + 1,)`:

   ```python
   phi = polynomial_features(x, degree)
   # np.linalg.lstsq(phi, y, rcond=None) returns a tuple. Keep item 0.
   ```

   The whole tuple is not the weight vector.

3. **`sweep_degrees(x_train, y_train, x_test, y_test, degrees)`** fits each degree on the training flats and returns two arrays, `train_risks` and `test_risks`, each of shape `(len(degrees),)`:

   ```python
   for degree in degrees:
       w = fit_polynomial(x_train, y_train, degree)
       # Score predict_polynomial(w, x_train) and predict_polynomial(w, x_test).
   ```

   Fit on the training flats only. Fitting on the test flats makes the held-out column meaningless.

4. **`dart_decomposition(hits, bullseye, noise_std)`** takes the guesses of several fits at one flat and returns `(bias_sq, variance, noise)` as Python floats:

   ```python
   bias_sq = (hits.mean() - bullseye) ** 2
   # variance: np.var(hits). noise: noise_std ** 2.
   ```

   `np.var` divides by the number of hits, which matches the 0.05 above. Dividing by one less gives 0.0667.

5. **`sample_training_set(true_function, noise_std, n_train, x_domain, rng)`** returns `(x, y)`, each of shape `(n_train,)`:

   ```python
   x = rng.uniform(low, high, size=n_train)
   # y = true_function(x) + noise_std * rng.normal(size=n_train)
   ```

   Draw the sizes first, then the noise, from the generator you are given. A generator created inside this function would restart its sequence, and every call would return the same flats.

6. **`bias_variance_decomposition(true_function, noise_std, x_query, degree, n_train, n_trials, x_domain, seed)`** returns three arrays of shape `(q,)`:

   ```python
   rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)       # one generator for the whole simulation
   predictions = np.empty((n_trials, len(x_query)))
   # Each row: sample_training_set, fit_polynomial, predict_polynomial at x_query.
   # Reduce on axis 0, as in the code above.
   ```

   Create the generator once, outside the loop. Making it inside the loop repeats the same training set in every trial.

## Where this breaks

**High powers are badly scaled.** In this market sizes lie between 0 and 1, so high powers shrink: $0.5^{12}$ is about 0.0002. The high-degree columns are nearly zero for most flats and nearly alike, so the solver needs enormous weights to make them matter. If size were in square metres instead, a 50 square metre flat would give $50^{12}$, about $2\times10^{20}$, in the same table as a column of ones. Either way the fit is fragile, so rescale the sizes before you use high powers.

**Too many weights for too few flats.** With 15 flats, a degree-14 polynomial can pass through every flat exactly, like the memoriser, so a training risk of 0 tells you nothing. Likewise, the held-out risks at degree 3 and degree 6 differ by only 0.008, so pick a simple degree near the bottom of the curve instead of the exact minimum.

**Real data has no true function.** In a simulation you know $f$. With real flats you cannot compute the bias directly, because the held-out risk measures only the sum of the three parts. More flats lower the variance and leave the bias nearly unchanged, so the best degree tends to rise as the training set grows.

## The exercise

You write `polynomial_features`, `fit_polynomial`, `sweep_degrees`, `dart_decomposition`, `sample_training_set`, and `bias_variance_decomposition`. The first checks confirm that the design matrix has one column per power and reproduces the guesses 9 and −1.5, that your weights recover an exact line and an exact quadratic with no nearby weights doing better, and that `sweep_degrees` matches an independent fit. The sweep must show training risks that never rise with degree, a held-out risk that falls and then rises, and a huge held-out risk with a tiny training risk when the degree nearly equals the number of flats.

The dart check verifies 0.01, 0.05, and 0.09, and the sampler check verifies that sizes stay in the domain, that the given generator is used, and that the noise has the right spread. The simulation checks come last. The noise term must equal `noise_std ** 2`, a fit of the right shape with no noise must show zero bias and zero variance, and a constant fit to $f(x)=x$ must match the exact bias $(0.5-x)^2$ and the exact variance $(1/12+\sigma^2)/n$. The three parts must add up to a separate direct estimate of the squared miss, and raising the degree from 1 to 7 must lower the bias and raise the variance.
