# Kernel ridge regression: a weighted sum of similarities

You have six noisy points along a curve and a kernel that scores how alike two inputs are. A query input arrives, and you must predict its target. The kernel tells you which training points resemble the query. It does not yet tell you how to turn those scores into a number.

Two quick answers come to mind. Copy the target of the most similar training point, and the prediction jumps whenever a different point becomes the closest one. Average the targets weighted by similarity, and the curve is smoother but flattened at the ends, where a point has neighbours on one side only. Neither follows the trend well, and neither gives you a dial for how closely to chase noisy points.

This chapter builds a better answer. The prediction is a weighted sum of similarities to the training points, and the weights come from one linear solve. The result is *kernel ridge regression*, ridge regression run with a kernel in place of explicit features.

## What you already know

- A kernel scores two points, and the Gram matrix $K$ holds the score for every pair of training points ("Kernels: a similarity score that hides a bigger feature space").
- A valid kernel has a Gram matrix with no negative eigenvalue ("What makes a similarity a valid kernel").
- Ridge regression adds a fee $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ for large weights and has a closed-form answer ("Ridge: a fee on large weights").
- A gradient is the vector of slopes, and a minimum of a smooth bowl has gradient zero.
- A Cholesky factorisation writes a symmetric positive definite matrix as $LL^{\top}$ with $L$ triangular.

## The picture: one bump per training point

Put a bump on every training point, scale each bump by a weight, and add them. The result is the model. Figure 1 shows it for the six points: the thin lines are the scaled bumps, and the orange curve is their sum.

Written as a formula, the prediction at a query input $x$ is

$$
\hat{y}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\,k(x, x_i) .
$$

Read it aloud: "for each training point, take how similar $x$ is to it, multiply by that point's weight $\alpha_i$, and add up." The weights are the unknowns. Everything else is a kernel evaluation you already know how to compute.

## Why not use the targets as the weights?

The natural weights are the targets themselves: let each point vote with its own $y_i$. This fails when points sit close together, and two points show it. Take $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$ with the RBF kernel at $\gamma = 0.5$, so the similarity between them is $0.6065$, and take targets $y = (1, 2)$.

With $\alpha = y$, the prediction at the first point is $1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0.6065 = 2.21$, though its target is 1. At the second it is $1 \cdot 0.6065 + 2 \cdot 1 = 2.61$, though its target is 2. Each point's bump spills onto its neighbour, so the two votes are counted twice. Crowded regions get over-counted, and the fix is to choose weights that account for the overlap.

## Choose the weights so the sum reproduces the data

Ask for weights at which the sum of bumps hits each target. The prediction at training point $i$ is $\sum_j K_{ij}\alpha_j$, so asking for all of them at once is the system $K\alpha = y$. For the two points that system has the solution $\alpha \approx (-0.337,\ 2.204)$, and it gives exactly $(1, 2)$. The first weight is negative, because it cancels the spill from the neighbour.

Exact reproduction is the wrong goal for noisy data. On the six-point example the weights from $K\alpha = y$ reach about $\pm 6$, and the curve swings wildly between points to hit every dot. Ridge regression met the same problem with large weights, and it has the same remedy: charge a fee for a large function, and let squared error trade against it.

The fee for the function is $\lambda\,\alpha^{\top}K\alpha$. It is the ridge fee $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ in disguise: if $w = \Phi^{\top}\alpha$ for feature rows $\Phi$, then $\lVert w\rVert^2 = \alpha^{\top}\Phi\Phi^{\top}\alpha = \alpha^{\top}K\alpha$. The objective is

$$
J(\alpha) = \lVert y - K\alpha\rVert^2 + \lambda\,\alpha^{\top}K\alpha .
$$

Read it aloud: "the squared miss of the sum of bumps, plus lambda times the size of the function." Set the gradient to zero. The gradient is $-2K(y - K\alpha) + 2\lambda K\alpha$, which factors as $2K\left((K + \lambda I)\alpha - y\right)$. It vanishes when

$$
(K + \lambda I)\,\alpha = y .
$$

Read it aloud: "the similarities of each point to the others, with lambda added on the diagonal, times the weights, equals the targets." This is $K\alpha = y$ with $\lambda$ added on the diagonal. For $\lambda > 0$ and a positive semidefinite $K$, the matrix $K + \lambda I$ has no zero eigenvalue, so the system has exactly one solution. It is a minimiser of $J$, and when $K$ is invertible it is the only one. Any other minimiser gives the same predictions.

## A worked example: shrinkage and borrowing

Use the two points with $\lambda = 1$. The system matrix is

$$
K + \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0.6065 \\ 0.6065 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det = 4 - 0.6065^2 \approx 3.632 .
$$

Solving gives $\alpha \approx (0.2167,\ 0.9343)$, and the predictions at the training points are $K\alpha \approx (0.7833,\ 1.0657)$. Figure 2 compares them with the targets.

## The dual view: weights on points or weights on features

For a linear kernel, $k(x, x') = x^{\top}x'$, kernel ridge regression must agree with ordinary ridge regression, and it does through the identity $w = X^{\top}\alpha$. Test it with two one-dimensional points, $x = (1, 2)$ with targets $(1, 2)$ and $\lambda = 1$. The Gram matrix is $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$, so $K + I = \begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&5\end{bmatrix}$ and $\alpha = (1/6,\ 1/3)$. Then $w = 1\cdot\tfrac16 + 2\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac56$. Ordinary ridge gives $w = (1\cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2)/(1 + 4 + 1) = \tfrac56$ as well.

The two routes solve different-sized systems. Primal ridge solves a $d \times d$ system, one weight per feature. Kernel ridge solves $n \times n$, one weight per training point. Prefer the primal form when $d$ is small and $n$ is large. Prefer the kernel form when $d$ is huge or infinite, as it is for the RBF kernel, and $n$ is moderate.

## Solving the system in numpy

$K + \lambda I$ is symmetric and positive definite, so factor it as $LL^{\top}$ and solve two triangular systems rather than forming an inverse. An inverse costs more and loses digits, and Cholesky also refuses a matrix that is not positive definite, so a broken kernel fails loudly rather than returning a silent wrong answer.

```python
L = np.linalg.cholesky(K + lam * np.eye(n))
z = np.linalg.solve(L, y)           # solve L z = y
alpha = np.linalg.solve(L.T, z)     # solve L^T alpha = z
```

Here `np.linalg.solve` treats the triangular factors as general matrices, so it does not get the speed-up of a true triangular solve. A routine such as `scipy.linalg.solve_triangular` does. The answer is the same.

To predict, build the matrix of similarities from each query to each training point, with one row per query, and multiply by `alpha`: `K_test @ alpha`.

> [!WARNING]
> `K_test` has shape `(n_test, n_train)`: queries on the rows, training points on the columns. Build it as `rbf_kernel(X_test, X_train, gamma)`. Swapping the arguments gives the transpose and a shape error or a wrong answer.

> [!KEY]
> Kernel ridge regression predicts with a weighted sum of similarities to the training points, and finds the weights from one linear system, $(K + \lambda I)\alpha = y$. It answers the opening question: the weights fix the over-counting that raw targets would cause, and $\lambda$ keeps the curve from chasing noise.

> [!RECAP]
> - The model is $\hat{y}(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(x, x_i)$, a scaled bump on each training point.
> - Setting the gradient of $\lVert y - K\alpha\rVert^2 + \lambda\alpha^{\top}K\alpha$ to zero gives $(K + \lambda I)\alpha = y$.
> - The training miss at point $i$ is $\lambda\alpha_i$, so $\lambda$ trades fit for smoothness, and $\gamma$ sets bump width.
> - A linear kernel gives $w = X^{\top}\alpha$, the ordinary ridge weights.
> - Solve with a Cholesky factor, and predict with `K_test @ alpha` where `K_test` is `(n_test, n_train)`.

## Build it step by step

The starter provides `rbf_kernel` from the previous exercise. Build three functions.

1. **`kernel_ridge_fit(K, y, lam)`**. `K` is `(n, n)` and `y` is `(n,)`. Return `alpha` of shape `(n,)`. Factor `K + lam * np.eye(n)` with `np.linalg.cholesky`, then solve twice. The usual mistake is adding `lam` to every entry of `K` instead of the diagonal.
2. **`kernel_ridge_predict(K_test, alpha)`**. `K_test` is `(n_test, n_train)`. Return `(n_test,)`. One matrix-vector product. The usual mistake is passing the transpose.
3. **`implied_weights(X, alpha)`**. For a linear kernel, return the feature-space weights `X.T @ alpha`, shape `(d,)`. The usual mistake is `X @ alpha`, which has the wrong shape.

## Cost and scaling

Building $K$ costs about $n^2 d$ operations, and the Cholesky solve costs about $n^3/3$. Doubling $n$ makes the solve eight times slower. Storing $K$ takes $n^2$ numbers, so at $n = 100{,}000$ with 8-byte floats that is about 80 GB. Each prediction costs $n$ kernel evaluations per query. The next chapter, "The representer theorem and random Fourier features", shows how to avoid the $n \times n$ matrix.

## Where this breaks

**A badly chosen $\gamma$.** Too large memorises, too small underfits. Pick $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ together with cross-validation.

**Unscaled features.** The RBF kernel uses distances, so a feature measured in thousands swamps one measured in fractions. Standardise each column first.

**An invalid kernel.** A similarity that fails the test from the previous chapter can make the Cholesky factorisation fail for small $\lambda$.
